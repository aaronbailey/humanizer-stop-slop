# Combined pattern catalog

Use this catalog to diagnose prose. Look for clusters and effects. Do not treat a watched word as proof of AI authorship or as an automatic deletion.

## Content and evidence

### Inflated importance

Watch for claims that ordinary events are pivotal, enduring, historic, transformative, or evidence of a broad trend. Replace the claim with the concrete event and its supported consequence.

Common signs: `pivotal`, `crucial`, `testament`, `indelible`, `marks a shift`, `reflects a broader`, `setting the stage`, and `shaping the landscape`.

### Prestige as a substitute for relevance

Lists of publications, institutions, awards, or follower counts may try to prove importance without explaining it. Keep only sourced context that helps the reader understand the subject.

### Shallow analysis

Trailing `-ing` phrases often decorate a fact with unsupported interpretation: `highlighting`, `showcasing`, `symbolizing`, `underscoring`, `ensuring`, or `reflecting`. Keep the interpretation only when the source supports it.

### Sales language

Remove travel-brochure and launch-copy adjectives when the genre does not call for them: `vibrant`, `breathtaking`, `renowned`, `must-visit`, `groundbreaking`, `rich`, `stunning`, `nestled`, and `in the heart of`.

### Vague attribution

Phrases such as `experts believe`, `observers note`, `industry reports`, and `some critics argue` hide the source. Name a source already present in the material or remove the unsupported attribution. Never invent one.

### Formulaic challenges and outlook

Stock sections about challenges, resilience, future prospects, or continued growth often add optimism without facts. Keep concrete problems, dates, plans, and actions. Cut the template around them.

## Language and agency

### Throat-clearing and announcements

Start with the content instead of announcing it.

Watch for:

- `Here's the thing`, `Here's what you need to know`, or `Here's why`
- `Let me be clear`, `The truth is`, or `The reality is`
- `Let's dive in`, `Let's explore`, or `Let's break this down`
- `Quick note`, `Heads up`, or `Before I forget`
- `In this section`, `As we'll see`, or `I want to explore`

Keep an announcement when it performs real navigation in a long or complex document.

### Inflated verbs and stock AI words

Prefer `is`, `has`, and a precise action when phrases such as `serves as`, `stands as`, `boasts`, `features`, `delves into`, `showcases`, or `underscores` add ceremony rather than meaning.

Words such as `additionally`, `align`, `crucial`, `enhance`, `foster`, `interplay`, `intricate`, `key`, `landscape`, `pivotal`, `quietly`, `tapestry`, `valuable`, and `vibrant` become tells when they cluster. Do not replace an accurate technical use merely because the word appears on this list.

### Filler, emphasis, and hedging

Compress phrases that do no semantic work:

- `in order to` to `to`
- `due to the fact that` to `because`
- `at this point in time` to `now`
- `has the ability to` to `can`
- `it is important to note that` to the underlying claim

Remove performative emphasis such as `full stop`, `let that sink in`, `make no mistake`, and `this matters because` when the following detail already establishes importance.

Keep one accurate qualifier. Stacks such as `could potentially possibly` usually collapse to `may`. Do not strengthen an uncertain claim by deleting a necessary qualifier.

### Adverbs

In strict-directness mode, challenge adverbs, especially empty intensifiers and sincerity markers such as `really`, `just`, `literally`, `genuinely`, `honestly`, `simply`, `actually`, `deeply`, and `truly`.

In natural and voice-match modes, keep adverbs that carry timing, degree, stance, technical meaning, humor, or the writer's recognizable cadence. Replace them only when a stronger verb or a concrete detail says more.

### Passive voice and missing subjects

Use active voice when it clarifies who acted. Passive voice is valid when the actor is unknown, irrelevant, obvious, deliberately withheld, or conventional in the genre. Never invent an actor.

Incomplete phrases such as `No configuration needed` often improve when the reader or system becomes the subject: `You do not need a configuration file.`

### False agency

Abstract nouns can hide the person responsible: `the decision emerged`, `the conversation moved`, `the market rewarded`, or `the data tells us`. Name the decision-maker or interpreter when the source identifies one. Otherwise state the observable event without fabricating a person.

### Synonym cycling and repeated openings

Use one stable name for the same subject instead of rotating among `the protagonist`, `the hero`, and `the central figure`. Merge or reshape repetitive sentence openings when they create a mechanical cadence. Keep deliberate repetition that builds rhythm or pressure.

## Structure and rhetoric

### False contrasts and negative runways

AI prose often sets up an easy reversal:

- `It's not X. It's Y.`
- `Not because X, but because Y.`
- `X isn't the problem. Y is.`
- `not just X but also Y`
- `It wasn't X. It wasn't Y. It was Z.`

State Y directly when X adds no real option, distinction, or objection. Keep contrasts that the argument genuinely needs.

### Forced groups of three

Use the number of items the meaning requires. A steady stream of three-part lists can sound generated. Do not delete a third item when it carries distinct information.

### False ranges

`From X to Y` should describe a real continuum. When the endpoints merely name unrelated topics, list the topics directly.

### Rhetorical setups and fake revelations

Watch for questions answered at once, `What if I told you`, `Think about it`, `At its core`, `The real question is`, `What really matters`, and `the deeper issue`. Remove the stagecraft and state the supported point.

### Fake objections and alternatives

Delete objections or options that no reader raised and the passage immediately dismisses. Preserve named objections, plausible design choices, safety caveats, FAQs, and real tradeoffs.

### Dramatic fragmentation and forced punchlines

One short sentence can work. A row of fragments or paragraph-ending slogans often manufactures intensity: `No preference. No prior. No nostalgia.` Combine the ideas into sentences whose lengths follow the content.

### Formulaic sayings and quotables

Rewrite lines shaped like slogans when they replace evidence: `X is the language of Y`, `X becomes a trap`, `the currency of`, or `the architecture of`. A memorable line may remain when it expresses a precise, supported point and fits the writer's voice.

### Distant narrator

Phrases such as `people tend to`, `nobody designed this`, or `this happens because` can float above the scene. Use a concrete actor, the reader, or a specific situation when the source supports that perspective. Do not force `you` into neutral reference prose.

### Sentence starters

Repeated `What`, `Why`, `How`, `Look`, or paragraph-opening `So` can become a tic. Restructure the pattern, not every occurrence. A natural question or necessary transition may stay.

## Formatting and presentation

### Dashes and punctuation

In strict-directness mode, replace ornamental em and en dashes with periods, commas, colons, or parentheses. In voice-match mode, follow the sample's punctuation habits. In natural mode, flag dashes only when they contribute to a formulaic rhythm.

### Decorative formatting

Remove excessive bolding, emoji labels, title-case headings, and lists whose bold mini-headings repeat the sentence. Keep formatting required by the medium, template, or user's request.

### Quotes and hyphens

Match the target format's quotation style. Curly quotes alone are not an AI tell. Keep grammatical hyphens before nouns and established technical terms; avoid piling up fashionable compound modifiers.

### Repeated headings and stale history

Delete a sentence that merely repeats its heading. In current documentation and comments, describe current behavior. Keep previous-version discussion in changelogs, release notes, migration guides, and historical explanations.

## Chatbot residue and endings

Remove greetings, praise, offers, and sign-offs that leaked into standalone prose: `Great question`, `Certainly`, `I hope this helps`, `Let me know`, or `Would you like me to`.

Replace knowledge-limit disclaimers and plausible guesses with a precise statement of what the source establishes. Omit the point if that statement adds no value.

Cut generic positive endings such as `the future looks bright`, `exciting times lie ahead`, or `a step in the right direction`. End on the last concrete fact, supported implication, or real next action.

## False-positive safeguards

Do not flag authorship based on polished grammar, formal vocabulary, dry prose, a single transition, one dash, one short sentence, curly quotes, standard templates, or missing citations. Protect quotations, titles, proper names, code, examples, and pre-2022 text from careless pattern matching.

Keep specific odd details, mixed feelings, dated slang, genuine asides, self-corrections, deliberate repetition, and uneven rhythm when they belong to the writer. These details often carry more voice than a perfectly smooth rewrite.
